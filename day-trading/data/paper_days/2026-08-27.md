## 2. NEW FINDING: the 0.5% spread cap is partly a tick-size artifact

One tick over price P is a relative spread of 0.01/P. Solving `0.02/P ≤ 0.005` gives
**P ≥ $4.00**: a name under $4 can never show a *two-tick* market inside the cap and must be
quoted **exactly one tick wide** to be armable at all. At $2.00 even one tick sits *at* the cap.

Today's evidence is unusually clean because two names were vetoed by the same rule measuring
completely different things:

| name | price | spread vetoed | in ticks | what the veto measured |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BTCT | 2.14 | 0.926%, 0.943% | **exactly 2 ticks** | the tick size |
| OKTA | 158.02 | 0.557% | **88 ticks** | genuine illiquidity |

The campaign's lead anomaly is that live vetoes 90–100% of premarket entries against a
modelled 50–65% optimum, and that gap has been blamed on threshold miscalibration. This says a
large part of it may be **mechanical**: the scan filters `Last > $2` and the gapper pool skews
cheap. The V-series modelled the veto on a *bar-range proxy*, which has no tick floor at all —
so the model could not have seen this.

**Testable prediction:** split the campaign's whole veto ledger at $4.00. Sub-$4 veto rates
should be far higher, and sub-$4 spreads should cluster at exact tick multiples rather than
spreading smoothly. This is *not* an argument to loosen the cap — it is an argument that the
cap is doing two different jobs and should be calibrated per price bucket.

## 3. Day 16's L2-vs-NBBO finding did not reproduce — and may be a measurement artifact

Day 16 recorded TH quoting 2.13% on `get_equity_price_book` and 0.785% on `get_equity_quotes`
"at the same second", a 2.7× gap, and flagged it as a candidate *mechanical* explanation for
the premarket veto rate. Today, two paired readings taken seconds apart agreed **exactly**:
OKTA 1.829% vs 1.829%, BTCT 0.943% vs 0.943%.

I nearly logged a divergence anyway. At 07:14:07 BTCT's NBBO read 0.459% (*inside* the cap);
at 07:15:13 its L2 read 0.943% (*outside*) — straddling the threshold, so it would have decided
the entry. Re-quoting at 07:15:23 returned 0.943%, identical to the book. The readings were
**66 seconds apart on a name moving 2.5% per minute**. There was no divergence at all.

Day 16's TH observation is timestamped "09:42–09:43", so it may have the same defect. **A
spread comparison across a one-minute gap on a gapper measures price movement, not feed
disagreement.** Treat that finding as unproven until reproduced with reads <5s apart on a
name that is not moving.

## 4. The operations were bad enough to have cost the whole day

Three self-inflicted outages totalling **4h01m**, every one from the same root cause: issue a
shell command, fail to verify it returned, lose the clock.

| gap | length | cost |
|---|---|---|
| 07:30–07:47 | 17 min | the 1-min poll declared at 07:30 never started |
| 08:36–09:28 | 52 min | **swallowed the market open**; the entry filled unobserved at 09:12 |
| 12:47–15:39 | **2h52m** | **blew the 15:00 flatten deadline** |

What saved the P&L was **architecture, not attention**: the Trigger B stop was armed at 08:27
with stop, limit, size, protective stop, scale-out and trail law all fully specified and
committed to git *before* the gap opened. That is precisely the case OUTAGE rule 2 exists for,
so the fill is settled from the tape rather than lost. Every armed exit rule was then replayed
over the third gap — lowest print 170.10 against a 150.742 stop — and nothing fired, so the
ticket settles on the 15:00 flatten.

**This is the strongest possible argument for resting orders over loop-layer decisions**, and
it should be read as a warning rather than a reassurance. A paper ledger can settle to 15:00.
A real account would simply still have been holding at 15:39. The one thing that cannot be
deferred is the exit, and today it was.

**The specific missing control:** MCP kept working through all three outages. Its response
timestamps are an *independent clock* and would have exposed the drift instantly at any point.
Next session: read the timestamp on every MCP response, treat any tool call over ~2 minutes as
shell failure, and from 14:30 drive the flatten off that independent clock — not off a shell loop.

## 5. Trigger C cadence, again

BTCT fired **four** buy_set signals premarket (07:06, 07:10, 07:11, and a **triple** —
bullish_engulfing + morning_star + tweezer_bottom — at 07:24). All four read STALE. None was
actually takeable, because BTCT was two ticks wide across the entire window and would have
been spread-vetoed at arming. But cadence, not the veto, is what refused them, and cadence is
not allowed to be the thing that refuses.

The honest resolution of Day 16's lesson: the 1-minute poll must track the **book**, not the
clock. Polling a name whose spread cannot clear the cap buys nothing; polling a name whose
book is tradeable is mandatory. Deviations from 1-minute were logged as they happened, with
their reasons and their risks, rather than discovered afterwards.
